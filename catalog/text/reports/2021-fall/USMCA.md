Optimal Industry Development Strategy for USMCA

0. Executive Summary

In this report, we uncover the economic path of the bloc of countries consisting of the United States, Mexico and Canada (USMCA), while comparing/contrasting development from the perspective of ECI to the individual countries that comprise the bloc. We also aim to understand how analyzing a bloc of countries (rather than the individual countries) changes suggested strategies for optimizing growth and complexity. We find that during the period of our interest, USMCA as a whole demonstrated a moderate level of economic growth with an average annual GDP growth rate of 3.32%, which falls right in between those of the US (3.39%) and Mexico (3.29%), with Canada lagging behind at 2.36%.

The lack of progress during the period is in line with the moderate/slow growth of the bloc’s constituent countries, and can be further explained through analysis of the economic and product complexity structure of USMCA on the industry group level. The product classification system we adopt allows us to measure the realized industry development for various industry groups and to observe that USMCA as a whole consistently relied on developing the Services and Utilities industry during the period, which is in line with the US and Mexico’s existing development paths.

Based on our analysis of the opportunity gain and a sensitivity analysis of major industry groups, this bloc can further its gains by prioritizing Transportation and High-Complexity Products. We further note that the USMCA has a slightly mismatched development strategy that indicates a moderately high (compared to other developed countries) level of structural optimality on the complexity level, having an adjusted R-square of around 1.2%. A significant positive correlation exists between five-year GDP growth and the structural optimality index of an economy as we define and calculate, but the USMCA still has a suboptimal growth experience. However, the bloc has the potential to further its economic gains through industrial structure optimization as suggested in the paper.

1. Report Overview

In this report, we aim to compare the economic path that the USMCA bloc went through during the period from 2013 to 2018 to those of its constituent country from the perspective of both Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and Economic Complexity Index (ECI), which reflect countries’ production capability and production structure, respectively. We then analyze the industrial framework and changes of this bloc as a whole during the period by comparing it to constituent countries on the basis of quantitative measures such as Product Complexity Index (PCI) and Product Family Complexity Index (PfCI), in addition to development strategies as quantitatively measured by opportunity gains on an industry-level. We conclude our report by comparing the macro-level results and predictions of this bloc, as deduced from regression models we have established, to the predicted path of individual constituents.

Clearly, as seen above, the ECI of USMCA does not increase much for different percentages of heuristic total export growth. In fact, at 5, 10, and 15 percent growths, we see a similar ECI change pattern, in that ECI is not increased at these growth rates. However, at a higher trajectory of 20%, we see a sharp change in suggested industries to allocate a budget to, as Transportation and High-Complexity Products become the primary industries for optimal development. This makes sense because with more budgets, a country should definitely focus more on the development of high-end product spaces.

4.3  Sensitivity Analysis and Contingency Strategy

In the following section our broad aim is to understand how sensitive the bloc of USMCA is to changes in export in various industries, especially relative to constituent nations. While we have seen how allocation strategies have changed drastically, we seek to uncover deeper ways of understanding how stable an ECI score can be in reality, as countries are rarely operating in isolation, but rather in trade blocs that can better sustain against specific kinds of economic shocks. We shall analyze this from both the perspective of the Sensitivity Graphs, as well as a Contingency Plan.

5. Macro-level Predictions

5.1 Structural Optimality Index

In order to quantitatively evaluate how effective our development strategies are and how deviations from the suggested developmental path will lead to suboptimal growth results, we define the measure – Structural Optimality Index (SOI) – on a country level as the Adjusted R-Square obtained from regressing the country’s realized industry development over the suggested development strategy. Both strategies are quantified in nature, with the realized development represented by export increase in every major industry group and the suggested development strategy represented by the opportunity gains of developing the corresponding industry groups as calculated in previous sections. The adjusted R-square from the regression thus provides us with a quantified measure of how the development path adopted by the country has fitted with/differed from calculations on a complexity level.

After obtaining this measure for all countries in our database, we further regress countries’ five-year economic growth, which is defined as the percent increase in GDP (USD), over their SOI. Figure 6 illustrates the regression output, which shows a strong positive correlation between countries’ SOI and their five-year GDP growth, significant at the 1% level and achieving an adjusted R2 of 4.5%. In other words, the more a country develops as suggested by calculations on a complexity level, the better economic outcomes it achieves.

Figure 2: Regression of Five-year GDP Growth over SOI

5.2 SOI of USMCA and Its Implications

The USMCA has a moderate level of structural optimality, much like its constituent countries. As the regression in Figure 7 suggests (the red curve represents export increase and the blue curve stands for opportunity gain of the corresponding industry), USMCA demonstrates a low level of correlation with the suggested developmental path, having an adjusted R-square of around 1.25%. As seen in the charts below, this is very much in line with constituent countries, which also do a poor job of following the suggested development path. In fact, Mexico has the lowest level of correlation within the set, and Canada has the highest. In sum, this bloc’s mixed economic progress from 2013 to 2018 can be at least partially explained by a development strategy that demonstrates a poor level of matches with our suggested developmental path. In specific, the USMCA as a whole invested heavily in the Transportation industry, while it should have focused more on more industry groups such as Services and Utilities and Precious Stones. That being said, it stayed fairly in line with our expectations in the Energy Drilling/Mining and Weapons industries.

6. Concluding Remarks

In conclusion, USMCA has demonstrated a low-moderate level of economic growth in terms of annual GDP increase during the period from 2013 to 2018, concurrent with a decrease in economic complexity as compared with other major developed regions.

Our analysis shows that the bloc as a whole behaves as a weighted structure of its constituents, and the resulting paths that we suggest are based upon this. We find that within the SDV, there is an increase in the number of positive-opportunity gain industries for both Mexico and the US when we look at the bloc as a whole. Additionally, we see that the allocation strategies as per our greedy algorithm are also in line with the majority of the constituent countries, but primarily the US (focus on High-complexity Products and Transportation). Finally, we note how sensitivities to industry shocks are magnified when we analyze the entire economic bloc. Additionally, industries which may be detrimental to the overall ECI of the bloc may not be as impactful on an individual country basis, as we see in the sensitivities, as well. Overall, we see that while there are certainly various paths for individual nations to optimize their ECI, alternative routes can be explored when we study entire blocs. Although they may not align to optimally enhance the ECI of any single nation, we see that by treating a bloc as a single nation, mutually beneficial paths are optimized.
