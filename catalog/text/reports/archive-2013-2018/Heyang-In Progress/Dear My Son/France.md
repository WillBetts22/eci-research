# Economic Report and Production Analysis for France

1. Report Overview

In this report, we aim to uncover the economic path France went through during the period from 2009 to 2014. Specifically, we dive into the topic through a macroeconomic overview of France from the perspective of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and Economic Complexity Index (ECI), which reflect countries’ production capability and production structure, respectively. We then analyze the industrial framework and changes of France during the period based on quantitative measures such as Product Complexity Index (PCI) and Product Family Complexity Index (PfCI), followed by a detailed analysis of how such changes have matched with development strategies as quantitatively measured by opportunity gains on an industry-level. We conclude our report with macro-level results and predictions of France, as deduced from regression models we have established on a global scale, which takes into account countries’ stances in their product space.

2. Macroeconomic Results

2.1 GDP Ranking and Changes

As the 6th largest economy in the world by GDP, France has a GDP of 2.7 trillion USD in 2009 and 2.8 trillion USD in 2014, exhibiting an extraordinary level of economic output. Figure 1 illustrates the time-series GDP results for world’s leading economies from 1996-2016, and France ranks among its counterparts as a stable player. France exhibits a consistent level of GDP during the twenty years from 1996 to 2016, while its annual growth rate is only about 1.1%. This rate is clearly below the annual growth rate of the group of leading economies in the world, which have a geometric average growth rate of 5.1% and an arithmetic average growth rate of 5.2%. The annual growth rate of 1.1% is particularly in sharp contrast to an annual growth rate of 15.5% for China, one of the world’s most important emerging economies and France’s competitor in many industries. The stagnated economic growth in France, we believe, can be reflected through consistent ECI and PCI, and can be further explained by a suboptimal set of production strategies France adopted during the period, which we will expand at the later stage of the report.

3. Production Analysis

3.1 Product Categorization

Our main data source in this research is the UN Comtrade Website (https://comtrade.un.org), which provides us with valuable information on countries’ export values on an annual basis, including highly specific export data for more than 5000 categories of products. In order to have a holistic vision of the product space of a country, however, a level of categorization is required. Product in our data source are labelled from 010000-999999, with the first two-digits representing a general category to which the product belongs. Based on the 99 categories as extracted from the label, we further group the products into 18 most widely-accepted industries, as summarized in Table 1.

Note that the High-complexity  industry group is not a distinct industry but rather an aggregated industry that encompasses the most complicated products from the most technologically-advanced industries (as measured by PCI). This industry serves as an indicator for us to quantitatively evaluate how well a country has performed in developing cutting-edge technologies and closing its technical gap with advanced economies, which has become a topic of wide concern today. Indeed, when we rank the 18 categories based on PfCI (an equivalent concept of PCI as calculated based on the 18 categorized product groups), High-complexity industry has the highest PfCI among all industry groups.

3.2 Actual Development

Based on the product categorization aforementioned, we aim to capture France’s actual production strategy between 2009 and 2014 on a complexity level. In Hausmann and Hidalgo (2009), distance is defined to be a measure of how far a country is from a certain product not in its product space. We extend this concept to all product groups and quantify how technically equipped a country is to develop corresponding product groups. In other words, the shorter the distance between a country and a product group, the more capable a country is in developing products within that group. Defining this net distance change as the Actual Developmental Index (ADI), our export data allows us to calculate and compare ADI of France and the 18 product groups in 2009 and 2014 through subtraction, where a positive ADI indicates a shortening of the distance (i.e., advancement of the industry), and a negative ADI indicates the reverse. As ranked in Table 2, during the period from 2009 to 2014, France has focused on products with a moderate level of complexity and those requiring intermediate skills, including raw manufacturing, mineral and stone, which is unusual among developed economies.

4. Product Space Design

4.1 Suggested Development Vector

As aforementioned, distance gives us an idea of how far each new product is from a country’s current mix of exports. As defined in Hausmann and Hidalgo (2009), distance is “the weighted proportion of products connected to goods p that country c is not exporting, i.e., MCP=0.”  Based on this definition, the distance between a country and any product that the country is currently exporting (i.e., the country is a significant exporter in the product as compared to global average) is zero. Similarly, opportunity gain quantifies the contribution of a new product in term of opening up the doors to more and more complex products. Intuitively, a product that a country is currently exporting (i.e., MCP=1) has an opportunity gain of zero. And unreasonable investment on certain product p would lead to a relative decrement in export of more profitable products, and therefore lead to a negative opportunity gain of product p.  The opportunity gain is not the only estimator of a country’s level of economic development. However, among countries with comparable economics strength, those having higher ECI, or in other words, more reasonable economics structure is often in better shape.

Table 5: Opportunity Gain Ranking on a Global Scale

We define the ranking of opportunity gain for a country c, as the Suggested Development Vector (SDV) of c. Table 5 summarizes how the SDV of France compares with a selected group of leading economies in the world, with the industry groups ranked in decreasing order of significance. Dark grey indicates positive opportunity gain, gray indicates zero opportunity gains, and light grey indicates negative opportunity gains. The result shows that leading countries barely have any industries with positive opportunity gain. In other words, those countries are already in the most stable and efficient economic structure. Without the necessity of economic transition, those countries merely need to maintain a steady and balanced economic development.  In contrast, African countries have nearly half of industries with positive opportunity gain, and share common interests in industrial manufacturing and high-tech industries (shown below in Table 5). It can be concluded that France, as a developing country, shares the characters of developed countries, with a balanced economics structure that leans towards high-end industries. At its current stage, France has no industries that are extremely under-developed and highly profitable.

4.2 Sensitivity Analysis and Contingency Strategy

Since the previous result shows that France is an advanced emerging economy with no obvious deficiency in terms of industry structure, a sensitivity analysis is performed by incrementally or decrementally altering the export of an industry by percentage, and calculating the correspondent change of ECI and SDV, in order to identify industries that have played particular important roles for an economy. For a given pair of country and industry, the sensitivity analysis aims to approximate the volatility of SDV subject to an instant increment or decrement of export and is mainly implemented on developed countries that have few industries with positive opportunity gains. In contrast, developing countries have positive opportunity gains in most of their industries and the result of sensitivity analysis is consistent with the first iteration of sequential development strategy, which is our primary tool for analyzing developing economies.

Since France assembles developed economies, as shown by prior results in section 4.1, sensitivity analysis is adopted here to better interpret its development. When the opportunity gain of the industry is negative, the sensitivity analysis approximates how an over-development of the industry jeopardizes the economic structure of a country. When the opportunity gain of the industry is zero, the sensitivity analysis determines whether the further development of the industry shapes the country a better economic structure, and predicts how the SDV would change accordingly.  Specifically, as we reduce the export of such an industry to 25%-50% of its original value, a few other industries originally with negative opportunity gain would probably become profitable. Instead of compensating for the decreasing export of the declining industry, the best strategy is to develop those incipient profitable industries.  In this manner, we can derive the contingency strategy for countries to deal with possible abrupt declines of crucial industries.  In some cases, the SDV remains unchanged even when a zero-opportunity-gain industry declines to its 20% in terms of export. We can then conclude the industry is not indispensable to the country.

5. Macro-level Predictions

5.1 Structural Optimality Index

Indeed, such choice of prioritization has proven to be a significant factor in deciding the economic outcomes of countries. In order to quantitatively evaluate how effective our developmental strategies are and how deviations from the suggested developmental path will lead to non-optimal growth results, we define the measure – Structural Optimality Index (SOI) – on a country level as the R-Square obtained from regressing the country’s actual development over the suggested development strategy. Both strategies are quantified in nature, with the actual development represented by export increase in major industry groups and the suggested development strategy represented by the opportunity gains of developing the corresponding industry groups as calculated in previous sections. The R-square from the regression thus provides us with a concrete measure of how the development path adopted by the country has fitted with/differed from our suggestion.

After obtaining this measure for all countries in our database, we further regress countries’ five-year economic growth, which as defined as the net increase in GDP (USD), over their SOI. Figure 6 illustrates the regression output, which shows a strong positive correlation between countries’ level of fitness and their five-year GDP growth, significant at the 1% level and achieving an adjusted R2 of 11.9%. In other words, the more a country develops as suggested by calculations on a complexity level, the better economic outcomes it achieves.

Figure 6: Regression of Five-year GDP Growth over SOI

5.2 SOI of France and Its Implications

France, for instance, has a relatively low level of structural optimality as compared with its developed peers. As the regression in Figure 7 suggests (the orange curve represents export increase and the blue curve stands for opportunity gain of the corresponding industry), France demonstrates a low level of correlation with the suggested developmental path, having an adjusted R-square of around 14.2%. France ranks the 34th place among all countries in terms of SOI and has an annual growth rate of merely 1.1% from 2009 to 2014, which is consistent with the regression result in Figure 6. This is in direct contrast to emerging economies such as China, where there is high SOI of around 82.8% and a corresponding GDP growth of 5.39 billion USD, making it one of the fastest developing economies during the period. In sum, France’s stagnant economic progress from 2009 to 2014 can be at least partially explained by a mismatched development strategy that demonstrates a considerable level of deviations from our suggested developmental path. In specific, France invested heavily in the transportation industry, while it should have focused more on industry groups such as service and machinery.

6. Concluding Remarks

In conclusion, France has demonstrated a relatively low level of economic growth in terms of GDP during the period from 2009 to 2014, concurrent with a stagnation in economic complexity as compared with other major developed economies in the world.

The lack of economic progress during the period, we believe, can be partially explained through analysis of the economic and product complexity structure of France on the industry group level. The product classification system we adopt allow us to measure the actual development for various industry groups and to observe that France invested heavily in raw manufacturing and stone industry groups during the period, while its developed peers and emerging economies around the globe have adopted much more complex and progressive paths.

Based on our analysis of the opportunity gain and a sensitivity analysis of major industry groups, France could further its gains by prioritizing high-tech, machinery and daily instrument. We further note that France has a slightly mismatched development strategy that indicates a moderate level of structural optimality on the complexity level, having an adjusted R-square of around 14.2%. Since a significant positive correlation exists between five-year GDP growth and the structural optimality index of an economy as we define and calculate, France indeed has a suboptimal growth experience as compared with fast-growing economies both on a regional scale and on a global scale, and has the potential to further its economic gains through industrial structure optimization as suggested in the paper.
