Does ECI improve out-of-time growth forecasts? Reconciling Will's Q16 and Julia's Project 3

Prepared 13 Nov 2026 for Will Betts and Julia Wang. Sources: willbetts/nightly/paper/eci_fourteen_tests.tex (Q16), juliawang/eci_growth_3.ipynb and juliawang/project3_horse_race.py. Reconciliation code: willbetts/forecast-reconciliation/.

Bottom line

The two results disagree because the specifications differ, and neither contains a coding error. Rerunning each person's code reproduces their published numbers exactly. Changing one specification choice at a time moves Will's result to Julia's.

  • The biggest driver is Will's extra control. His baseline includes the share of exports in ores, fuels and precious metals at the start year. Adding that control to Julia's designs cuts ECI's average gain from +4.3% to +1.2%. Removing it from Will's design turns −1.8% into +0.7%.
  • The second driver is which years are tested. ECI does worse than the baseline for forecasts starting 2009–2012 under either specification. Julia's three 10-year tests start in 2005, 2008 and 2014, so they skip those years.
  • Outcome units matter a little. PPP (Julia) favours ECI slightly over constant US$ (Will), but only once the resource-share control is gone.

Consolidated finding: adding ECI changes 10-year growth forecast error by a few percent at most, in either direction. Whether that change is positive depends on whether the resource composition of exports is already controlled for, and on which years are tested. Neither result supports a strong claim. Julia's "small, not established gain" and Will's "no improvement" are both fair readings of the same weak signal. Will's "every variant makes forecasts worse" is too strong.

Will reported

No complexity variant beats income plus resources. HS4 ECI −1.8%, Harvard published ECI −7.0%, diversity −2.3%, entropy −6.3%, Fitness −0.6%, log(1+RCA) ECI −5.7%. He called this "the strongest evidence against using ECI as an optimization target."

Julia reported

HS4 ECI beats the baseline in 7 of 8 designs (+3.5% to +9.6%, with one at −2.6%). Harvard ECI ranges from −5.9% to +20.4%. Her 95% intervals all include zero, so she calls the advantage over baseline and over diversity "not established."

Step 3: Results depend on the forecast start year

This is HS4 ECI's forecast gain for each 10-year origin under both specifications, with all other settings held fixed. Under both, ECI helps for forecasts starting 2005–2008 and 2013–2014 and hurts for forecasts starting 2009–2012. Those decades start near the commodity-price peak. We have not tested that as the cause, but it fits Will's Project 1 finding that windows starting 2010–2012 are the weakest for ECI.

Will averages over all ten origins, so the four weak years pull his total below zero. Julia's three 10-year origins are all good years for ECI, although they were chosen in advance and not by looking at the results. Neither choice is wrong. They show that a single "ECI helps / doesn't help" verdict hides large swings from one decade to the next.

Which specification is right?

Both answer legitimate questions, but different ones.

  • Julia: "Does ECI add forecasting information beyond income and resource rents?" Answer: a little, about +2% to +5%, and not distinguishable from zero.
  • Will: "Does ECI add information beyond income, rents and how commodity-heavy the export basket is?" Answer: no. The resource share is computed from the same export data and correlates −0.32 to −0.66 with ECI (by start year), so it already carries much of what ECI knows.

Will's own Project 1 follow-up (willbetts/project1/README.md) already found that this control "is largely a second measure of low complexity" and corrected the Q12 "fading effect" claim because of it. Q16 uses the same control and was never corrected. The Atlas instead controls for the change in resource exports during the decade. That control is not available at forecast time, so it cannot be used in an honest out-of-time test.

Suggested wording for the lab

ECI computed at HS4 is a sound measure of export complexity. In honest out-of-time tests it improves 10-year growth forecasts by at most a few percent, and only in some decades. The improvement disappears once the commodity share of exports is controlled for. The evidence supports describing ECI as a summary of a country's export capabilities. It does not support treating higher ECI as a reliable growth lever.
